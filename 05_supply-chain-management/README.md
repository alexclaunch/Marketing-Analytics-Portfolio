Across ten weeks, this course built a data-driven supply chain project around **global food waste (2019)**, working from the raw FAO / Our World in Data dataset toward a final strategy proposal. What this folder really documents isn't a single deliverable — it's *how the thinking changed.* The project ran through the **DMAIC** improvement cycle more than once, and the most valuable work happened in the loops back.

## DMAIC as the backbone

The whole project was structured around **DMAIC** (Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control):

- **Define** — Frame global food waste as a supply chain problem worth solving, and decide what "better" would actually mean.
- **Measure** — Source and clean the 2019 dataset, documenting the rationale behind every removal rather than just dropping rows.
- **Analyze** — Examine distribution, variance, and coefficient of variation to find where the real leverage is — testing whether apparent patterns were signal or reporting artifact.
- **Improve** — Translate the analysis into a concrete supply chain strategy and proposal.
- **Control** — Recognize the work doesn't end at a recommendation; it has to be monitored and revisited over time.

## Rethinking, not just running the steps

DMAIC looks linear on a slide. In practice, this project spent ten weeks circling back.

**The target kept moving.** The professor changed the specifics of what he wanted several times over the term, so a real part of the work was staying adaptable — constantly pivoting and reframing the approach instead of locking in early. Each shift in the brief meant revisiting earlier decisions: what's actually in scope, what question are we really answering, and what does "done" look like this week versus last.

**What does the number even mean?** A lot of the ten weeks went into *pondering interpretation* rather than computation — whether a "high waste" figure reflected genuine inefficiency, reporting differences, or just the structure of a country's food system. Cleaning the raw dataset down to a comparable set of countries forced the same question over and over: are these actually comparable, or am I averaging across things that don't belong together?

**The proposal was drafted and re-drafted.** The work moved from a mid-point framing to a final presentation, and the gap between the two is where the strategy matured as the analysis sharpened.

## What I took away

The biggest lesson — reinforced by the final exam — is that **these problems rarely have a perfect, closeable solution.** Food waste across a global supply chain is moving and multi-causal, and the assignment itself kept evolving. DMAIC isn't a one-time fix you run once and walk away from; its real value is the discipline of measuring, analyzing, and then *continuing to keep tabs* — and being willing to go back and rethink an earlier step when the data, or the brief, no longer supports where you landed. Accepting that there isn't always a tidy answer — and that the ongoing attention is itself the answer — was the most useful thing I learned all term.
